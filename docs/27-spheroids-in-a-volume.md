# 27. Spheroids in a volume

Chapter 25 set the model's spheroid against hundreds of measured ones through a disc, and left two things it could not do: compare a ball with a ball, and stop. The measured spheroids stop growing at 340 to 356 µm; the model's never stopped, because its dead core never lost any volume. This chapter grows the spheroid in a volume (chapter 26), gives dead tissue a way to break down, and sets both against the same spheroids.

## In the body

**A necrotic core loses mass.** Cells that die of anoxia in the middle of a spheroid do not stay as they are. Their membranes break, their contents are broken down, and what is small enough to pass leaves the core as fluid. Greenspan (Studies in Applied Mathematics, 1972) put this in the first model of a spheroid that stops: growth at the rim, loss in the core, and a size at which the two balance.

**What arrests the inner cells.** Before cells die in a spheroid's middle they stop dividing, alive but held in the first gap of their cycle (G1). In Greenspan's model, and in Browning and colleagues' fit of it, what holds them is "a chemical inhibitor that originates from the metabolic waste of living cells": every living cell releases it, it diffuses, the medium washes it away at the surface, and a cell arrests where it rises past a critical level, which is deepest in the middle and spreads outwards as the spheroid grows. Oxygen decides where cells die. In melanoma spheroids the arrested inner cells carry more p27 (p27Kip1), a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, one of the proteins that stop a cell's cycle in G1 (LaRue and colleagues, Cancer Research, 2004).

**What the dead release.** Necrosis is not only an end. Freyer (Cancer Research, 1988) grew spheroids of 15 cell lines and found that the size at which each stopped growing followed the size at which its necrosis first appeared. Extracts of spheroids with extensive necrosis stopped cells in culture from growing and killed them, the more of the extract the more so, where extracts of spheroids without necrosis did little; the activity was in the medium the large spheroids had grown in as well, so that it passes out through the spheroid. His conclusion: growth saturation in spheroids is regulated by factors "produced, released, or activated during the process of necrosis". Jiang and colleagues (Biophysical Journal, 2005) built a model of the kind this one is, a cellular Potts lattice with a protein network in each cell and diffusing fields, in which necrotic cells release growth and viability inhibitors that arrest and kill, and found by fitting it that such factors diffuse at $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-7}$ cm² an hour, as a protein of 80 to 90 kilodaltons (kDa) would, against $5.9 \times 10^{-2}$ for oxygen. Not to be confused with tumour necrosis factor (TNF), a cytokine of immune cells.

**The medium around a spheroid is still.** A spheroid in a well takes its oxygen from the medium around it, and in a well nothing stirs the medium: oxygen reaches the spheroid's surface by diffusing through it, and the medium next to the surface is depleted, the more so the more the spheroid takes up. Mueller-Klieser and Sutherland (British Journal of Cancer, 1982) measured the partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) at the surface of 21 spheroids of EMT6/Ro cells, from a mouse mammary sarcoma, kept in flowing medium: about 0.85 of the medium's at 450 µm across, about 0.6 past 1,000 µm; "smaller spheroids tend to have a higher Po2 at their surface". Mueller-Klieser (Biophysical Journal, 1984) traced it to an unstirred layer of medium next to the spheroid, 100 to 200 µm thick and "enlarging with increasing spheroid size", which "cannot be eliminated even through vigorously stirring the medium next to the spheroid". Browning and colleagues' spheroids grew in a flat-bottomed 96-well plate, on 50 µL of agarose under 200 µL of medium, which nothing stirs. Oxygen diffuses at $1.75 \times 10^{-5}$ cm² a second in tumour tissue at 37 °C (Grote and colleagues, Pflügers Archiv, 1977), and Place and colleagues (Free Radic Biol Med, 2017) take $2.69 \times 10^{-5}$ for complete culture medium at 37 °C, from $2.84 \times 10^{-5}$ measured in saline: 1.54 times faster in the medium than in the tissue.

**The tissue closes over what the core loses.** In Greenspan's model the tissue is packed and moves as one: what the core loses, the cells around it move in to fill, pushed by the rim's growth and held to each other, and the spheroid stays a solid ball rather than hollowing out. The rim's growth then goes into filling the core as much as into pushing the surface out.

**The balance has a rate.** In Greenspan's model as Browning and colleagues (eLife, 2021) use it, "cell debris is lost from the necrotic core at the per-volume rate λ", and what decides where growth stops is that rate against the rate at which cycling cells grow, their ratio $\gamma = \lambda / s$. Once the spheroid has stopped, what the rim adds is what the core loses:

$$1 - \phi^3 = \gamma \eta^3$$

with $\phi$ the radius of the arrested region over the outer radius and $\eta$ the radius of the necrotic core over it.

**The measured spheroids' end.** WM983b spheroids seeded from 5,000 and 10,000 cells reach 353 and 356 µm, and from 2,500 cells 340 µm, with "a necrotic core of 73% of the outer radius and an inhibited region of 90% of the outer radius, indicating a proliferative periphery approximately 35 µm (two to three cell diameters) thick".

**Their pace before it.** The same authors filmed every spheroid's outline as it grew. Between 200 and 320 µm the median radius of the WM983b spheroids grows at a steady pace, 13.5 to 14 µm a day for those seeded from 1,250 to 5,000 cells and 11.4 for those from 10,000, then slows over a few days to its end.

## In cellweave

**Debris loss.** A configuration's `debris_loss` is the share of a dead cell's volume its debris loses per step. From the step it dies a cell keeps to the volume it holds and is the dead tissue's, which the living may close over at once, and each step after the dead tissue owes the fluid that share of every dead cell's volume. Once it owes a whole pixel or more, the living tissue closes in over that many pixels at the edge of the dead tissue (`CpmLattice::close_over`): each is drawn at random from the run's generator among the dead pixels that touch living tissue, and goes to one of those living neighbours, drawn at random too, never to the medium; once the edge is taken, the dead pixels it laid bare are drawn in turn, up to eight times a step. The living cell that takes a pixel holds one more than it tries to, and presses on its neighbours, which press on theirs, until the pressure reaches the medium around the tissue, where a cell at the surface gives a pixel up, or reaches a growing cell, which takes it in place of one from the medium. The dead cell tries to hold that much less. What the core loses, the tissue gives up at its surface. Left out, nothing changes: a dead cell loses a pixel only when a neighbour takes it, and every run is the run it was.

The resorption of chapter 4, a dead cell asking each step to be two pixels smaller, works where a dead cell touches medium or a living cell that will take its pixels, as at the edge of a cord. In the middle of a spheroid every neighbour of a dead cell is another dead cell, asking to be smaller too, and nothing is lost: chapter 25 counted 0, 0 and 3 of some 800 dead cells resorbed to nothing by step 625. Debris loss acts where the debris is.

**A cycle of its own.** Without one, a cell divides once it has grown to 1.4 times its reference volume, and in a tissue grown from one cell every cell is checked at the same steps, every 20 (chapter 4). In the plane a cell takes longer than that to grow back, and the checks only round its cycle up. In a volume, swept four times a step, a daughter grows back within a few steps: every cell is ready at every check, the whole tissue divides at once, and the checks are the cycle. A configuration's `[cycle]` gives each cell a cycle that takes time (`Cycle`): when it is born, a cell draws how many steps its cycle lasts, from a log-normal distribution with a `median` and a `spread`, the standard deviation of the duration's logarithm, and it divides only once those steps have passed and it has grown enough. It grows through its cycle: a daughter is born at about half her mother's volume, and what she asks to be rises from half of what her network asks for to all of it, in proportion to how much of her cycle has passed (`grown`), where without a cycle she asks for all of it at once. The tissue is then checked every step, each cell against its own clock. Left out, nothing is drawn and every run is the run it was.

**Contact to the corners.** In the lattice of chapter 1 two pixels touch across a face, and a boundary costs one J for each pair of neighbours across it. A boundary along an axis of the lattice then costs one J for each pixel of its surface, and one along a diagonal more, in a volume up to 1.7 times as much: a tissue that settles towards its cheapest shape squares into a cube, faces along the axes, corners on the diagonals. A configuration's `contact_order` counts pixels as touching further out (`CpmLattice::set_contact_order`): at 3, in a volume, across a face, an edge or a corner, the 26 voxels around a voxel. Each contact is divided by 9, the number a voxel makes across a boundary along an axis, so that such a boundary costs what it cost and the others nearly as much: a ball's boundary costs 0.93 times its area, where across faces alone it cost 1.37 times. Copies are still proposed across faces alone: proposed from the 26 neighbours too, they let a spheroid lean into an octahedron and the bath into its core sooner (below). Left at 1, nothing changes and every run is the run it was.

**Fluid shut in drains.** Medium the bath reaches from the edge of the box is the bath, held at its oxygen (chapter 3). Medium the tissue closes in on every side is not: it is fluid in a cavity, and in Greenspan's model what the core loses leaves the spheroid through the tissue, which does not hollow out. With a debris loss, each step, every medium pixel the medium around the tissue cannot reach across faces (`CpmLattice::shut_in`) and that touches living tissue across a face is handed to a living neighbour drawn at random (`CpmLattice::close_in`), one layer of the cavity a step. The cell that takes the space presses on its neighbours until the pressure reaches the surface, as for the debris. Without a debris loss nothing is looked at or drawn.

**A waste that arrests.** A configuration's `[waste]` gives the living a waste to release (`Waste`): each step every pixel of a living cell releases its `production`, the dead and the medium release none, it diffuses as oxygen does, and the medium the bath reaches holds it at zero (`settle_waste`). Each cell's network reads the waste around it, its mean over the cell's pixels, as the input `waste`. What the cell does with it is the network's to say: in `examples/networks/ras-erk-waste.toml` a node p27, produced by HIF-1α as fast as it decays and switched on by the waste, steeply around a level of 1, takes the role HIF-1α held, the node whose height stops a cell's cycle (chapter 4). Where there is no waste p27 copies HIF-1α, and hypoxia arrests a cell where it did. A gained link (chapter 9) still reads one of the four inputs there were before (`REWIRED_INPUTS`), so that a run that rewires is the run it was. Left out, there is no field and every cell reads zero.

**A factor the dead release.** A configuration's `[necrotic_factor]` gives the dead a factor to release (`NecroticFactor`): each step every pixel of a necrotic cell releases its `production`, for `release_for` steps after the one it died at, as Jiang and colleagues had a necrotic cell release theirs for the 24 hours after it died, or for as long as it is there when that is left out (`died_at` keeps when each dead cell died); the living and the medium release none, it diffuses as the waste does, and the medium the bath reaches holds it at zero (`settle_necrotic_factor`, which shares `settle_released` with the waste). Each cell's network reads it, its mean over the cell's pixels, as the input `necrotic_factor`. A term `damaged_above` damages a node past a level of its source, offset by nothing, as `damaged_below` does below one: in `examples/networks/ras-erk-necrotic.toml` BCL2 is damaged past a level of 1 of the factor, as fast at twice that level as at no oxygen at all. Held at equilibrium, as every field here is, the factor depends only on its production over its diffusion, and the production is what a configuration sets. Left out, there is no field and every cell reads zero.

**A still medium.** A configuration's `[still_medium]` makes the bath's medium still (`StillMedium`), oxygen diffusing through it at its `diffusion`, in the units of the tissue's own, 0.2 a step (`OXYGEN_DIFFUSION`): 0.307 for the two measured diffusions, more where the medium moves; the example's 0.606 is set against the measured end (**Full size, still**, below). The box is too small to hold the medium of a well, and is not asked to: a still medium held at the source's level far away, taking up nothing, sits around a ball of radius $R$ that takes up $F$ in all at $1 - F / (4 \pi D R)$ at the ball's surface, $D$ its diffusion, and that is the level the bath holds the medium at next to the tissue (`Simulation::bath_level`). $F$ is read from the settled field, what the held medium hands the tissue across every face between them (`ScalarField::inflow`), and $R$ is the radius of a ball of every voxel the bath does not reach, the tissue and the fluid shut in it (`settle_bath_level`). The level is set from one step's field and held the next. Greenspan's relation between a spheroid's radius and its core's becomes, with $k$ the tissue's diffusion over the medium's, $R^2 \left(1 - 3\eta^2 + 2\eta^3 + 2k (1 - \eta^3)\right) = R_c^2 (1 + 2k)$: a core forms at a radius smaller by $\sqrt{1 + 2k}$ at the same uptake, and grows faster once it has. Only in a volume, around a spheroid in a bath, and without a waste or a necrotic factor, which would gather in a still medium as oxygen is depleted in it and which are not solved there; left out, the medium is held at 1 up to the tissue, as if stirred, and the run is the one it was.

**The spheroid in a volume**, `examples/spheroid-3d.toml`, grows from one ball of 123 voxels in a box 320 voxels on a side, in a bath whose medium is still.

## A voxel, and the oxygen

What follows is how the first runs were set. The example now takes a voxel of 2.975 µm and an uptake of 0.00017 with the medium stirred, once a cell grew through its cycle and held less (**The cells are smaller**, below), a loss of 0.024 a step, the one Greenspan's model asks of the measured end (**The loss the measured spheroids ask for**, below), and a still medium at 0.606, $k$ 0.33, with the uptake divided by $1 + 2k$ to keep the core's onset where it was, 0.0001024 (**Full size, still**, below).

**A voxel is about 2.8 µm.** Chapter 25 took a melanoma cell, with the space between cells, as a ball 18.7 µm across. In a test spheroid in a volume the cells held 157 voxels each, the tissue's volume over its cells, 6.7 voxels across as a ball: 2.8 µm a voxel. While the cells divide together (chapter 26) a cell holds between about 90 and 180 voxels, between 2.6 and 3.3 µm a voxel.

**The uptake is the plane's, carried over, not fitted again.** Chapter 25 fixed the uptake in the plane so that a disc forms its core where a sphere with the spheroids' oxygen would, at 237 µm. In a volume nothing needs converting: a ball's core is where the sphere formula puts it (chapter 26). At 2.8 µm a voxel, 237 µm is 85 voxels, and the uptake that puts a ball's core there is $6 D \times 0.97 / 85^2$, 0.00016 in voxel units: the plane's uptake against diffusion, a length of about 100 µm either way, measured in voxels instead of pixels.

Predicted before the runs, written while the first had no dead cell: (a) the core appears at 85 voxels, 239 µm, give or take 15; (b) the ratio of the radius at which arrest appears to the radius at which the core does, $R_q / R_c$, 0.84 give or take 0.05, as in the plane, since it is the ratio of two oxygen thresholds and does not depend on the dimension; (c) the rim of cycling cells past 1.34 $R_c$ still at least twice the spheroids' relative to their core, above 0.32 $R_c$, since arrest is still oxygen alone.

Read every 5 steps around the onset:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| WM983b spheroids | 237 µm | 207 µm | 0.87 | 37 µm, 0.16 $R_c$ |
| the spheroid in a volume | 239 µm, 85 voxels | 205 µm, 73 voxels | 0.86 | 91 µm, 0.38 $R_c$ |

(a) **Held**: no dead cell at step 295, at a radius of 85.0 voxels; ten at step 300, at 85.5. (b) **Held**, at 0.86. Neither was fitted: the uptake came from the plane, the voxel's size from the cells. (c) **Held**: the plane had 0.40 (chapter 25), the volume 0.38. The rim is as thick in a ball as in a disc, so the dimension was not what made it thick; what arrests a cell, oxygen alone, is.

At the centre of a ball of 130 voxels whose core forms at 85, oxygen cannot be above zero unless something inside holds it up. The probe with the tissue's surface twice and three times as costly, J(medium, tumour) 32 and 48 against 16 (in a copy of the code, the table being no configuration's), and with λ at 10 against 50, let the bath inside 0.8 of the radius sooner, not later: 21,235, 32,196 and 34,083 voxels of it at step 360, against 20,613.

**A cell that grows through its cycle.** A daughter is born at half her mother's volume and, until now, asked at once for all of her mother's target. The probe grew more slowly before its core, 449,930 voxels at step 220 against 535,803; across faces it squared and opened as before, and to the corners it still opened, later and less: 769 voxels of reached medium and 2,231 shut in at step 360.

**A quantum against a surface.** Every reading agrees on one thing: the ring on the dead tissue holds a voxel or so above its target, and a voxel over its target is worth $2\lambda$, 100, to a cell that gives it up to medium. It goes, one cell at a time, to the first medium it meets; the outer surface takes most, but a valley nearer to the core takes more than its share and deepens, as the front of something dissolving fingers. What holds such a front is its surface tension, Greenspan's point, here 16 a contact against 100 a quantum. ## Choices, and why

**The living tissue closes in at the edge of the dead tissue.** Three versions of debris loss were run before this one, and none could stop a spheroid at any rate:

- **Pixels turned to medium where they lay.** The core hollowed out: in a probe run, a box 140 voxels on a side at an uptake of 0.0005 and a loss of 0.16 a step, 31,084 voxels of medium the bath could not reach against 43,890 dead by step 253, and the outer surface grew as if nothing were lost. Only cycling cells grow, at the surface, into the bath, and nothing in the lattice pushes a tissue into a hole in its middle. At the full size, losses of 0.06, 0.1 and 0.16 a step reached the edge of a box 320 voxels on a side at steps 446 to 448, at radii within 1.5% of each other.
- **Each pixel handed to a neighbour across a face.** No cavity, but a dead cell inside the core has only dead neighbours: it handed them its pixels and received theirs, and the dead cells came to hold 408,687 voxels more than they tried to, out of 543,968. The loss the core could give up grew with its surface, not its volume, and three times the rate made almost no difference.
- **Pixels taken at the edge, one sweep a step.** The loss then acted, 7.5% less tissue by step 280 at a loss of 0.16, but the pressure it put into the living cells at the core's edge reached the surface too slowly, piling up as 127,073 voxels above the living cells' targets, and the spheroid still reached the edge.

So the debt of the whole dead tissue is paid where the living tissue meets it, which in Greenspan's balance is what a loss anywhere in the core comes to: the necrotic edge moving in at $\lambda \eta R / 3$ a step.

**Never to the medium.** The first version of this one handed a dead pixel to a medium neighbour as readily as to a living one, and every medium pixel the bath reaches from the edge of the box is held at its oxygen (chapter 3). Between two rounds of division the tissue swings by a fifth to a third, and medium comes in from the surface where it parts; a dead pixel touching it became medium, the dead pixel behind it was drawn at the next pass, and a channel of bath, open to the outside, bored into the core. In probe runs at a loss of 0.32 a step, 87,611 voxels of medium lay inside 0.8 of the radius by step 340, as deep as 0.43 of it; at the full size the lowest oxygen any living cell saw rose from 0.018 at step 320 to 0.191 at step 400, at the centre of a ball whose core forms at 85 voxels. Cells there stop dying, the core stops making debris, and the ball grows. Fluid in a core is not the bath: what the core loses, only the living tissue closes over.

**Several sweeps a step, in a volume.** Everywhere else a step of the lattice is one Monte Carlo sweep, and the plane's clocks were calibrated with one (chapters 12 and 25). A core breaking down asks the tissue to carry out to its surface, each step, as much as the core loses, one pixel moved across one cell boundary at a time, and with one sweep a step the pressure piled up. A configuration's `sweeps_per_step` sweeps the lattice several times between two updates of the networks and the field: a packed tissue's mechanics are fast against its growth, a cell's shape settling in minutes where its cycle takes a day. With four sweeps the cells held less than a thousandth of the tissue above their targets, and between two rounds of division the tissue now shrinks.

**Contact counted to the corners, each contact divided by 9.** A wider contact can be weighed two ways: keep each contact's J, so that every boundary costs more, or divide it so that a boundary along an axis costs what it cost. The second keeps the tension of a flat surface, the one property of the contact energies earlier chapters leaned on, and changes only how it depends on direction. Each copy attempted sums 26 neighbours instead of 6, but most are inside a cell and refused before anything is summed.

**Copies across faces, whatever the contact.** Proposed from the 26 neighbours, the copies take the direction out of how fast a boundary moves as well as out of what it costs, and in the probes they held the bath out longest. But however the contacts were weighed the tissue leaned into an octahedron, and at full size the bath came in sooner. Proposed across faces, the copies are those of every other chapter.

**A cell grows from half its target, in a straight line.** A daughter holds about half of what her mother held when she divided, so half of what her network asks for is where her target starts; it reaches all of it when her cycle has run, as the mother's had when she divided. How a real cell's volume rises through its cycle, in a straight line or faster towards its end, is argued; a straight line is the simplest that does not ask at birth for what a cell will be at division.

**The fluid drains one layer a step.** Handing a whole cavity to the tissue around it in one step would ask it to carry out, at once, as much as the core lost in many. One layer a step closes a pocket while it is a few pixels, before it is a cavity. Medium a channel connects to the bath is left alone: it is the bath, and only the tissue closing over it ends it.

**Structure against size, then the end.** As in chapter 25, the onset of the core and of the arrest are compared at the same radius, not the same time. The end is compared at the measured spheroids' largest radii, the latest day each seeding was imaged at its largest, as Browning and colleagues do.

**The still medium solved around a ball, not in the box.** Solved in the box and held at its edge, the medium's depletion would depend on how far the edge is, as the tissue's oxygen did before the bath (chapter 3), and a box large enough for a well's medium is millions of voxels more for nothing that happens there. Around a ball the solution is exact, and a spheroid is nearly one; a tissue that is not round is given the level of a ball of its volume. A plane is refused: around a disc a still medium has no level to settle at, its depletion growing with the logarithm of how far the medium reaches, and the plane's spheroid (chapter 25) keeps a stirred bath. The level is set a step late because what the tissue takes up is set by its living cells, which a step changes by a few in a hundred, and a cell a lower level starves takes many steps to die; solving the two together would solve the field several times a step. The well's own depth, which lowers the level further, is left out; any convection in the medium, which raises it, is folded into the medium's diffusion, as a faster diffusion would carry oxygen to the surface, which is why the example's is above the molecular one.

**One engine, two geometries.** The plane is kept exactly as it was: without `debris_loss`, nothing in this chapter draws a number or moves a pixel, and every example gives the same output, byte for byte.
